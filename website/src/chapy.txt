Your visa concern is important, but there is one misconception I'd correct

That is not quite as absolute as that.

If your status is something like Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services, losing your job does not automatically mean your residence status disappears the next day.

There are notification requirements when your employment/contract with the relevant organization ends or changes. For example, Immigration Services Agency guidance says that people with statuses including Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services must notify immigration within 14 days when their contract with the relevant organization ends or a new contract begins.

There is also a rule concerning failure to engage in the activities covered by your status for an extended period. Immigration guidance discusses the three-month threshold in the context of possible revocation, while also recognizing that there can be justifiable reasons, including circumstances involving job searching.

So it's not simply:

No job = immediately lose visa.

That's actually a surprisingly difficult question.

Instead ask:

"What problems have I solved?"

Then:

"Which problems did I personally take ownership of?"

Then:

"What changed because I did it?"

That's much easier.

For example:

Weak internal thought

"I just changed the Azure Pipeline."

Better interpretation

"The pipeline had a manual/inefficient process. I investigated how it worked, identified an opportunity for automation, implemented the change, and reduced the amount of manual work required."

Same work.

Completely different interview answer.

And don't make the mistake of saying "it was easy"

This is particularly important.

Suppose you automated something and it took you two hours.

You might think:

"That's nothing. It was easy."

The company doesn't care that it was easy for you.

If the previous process required developers to manually do something every sprint, and you eliminated that work, that's valuable.

In fact, being able to solve something easily can be a positive sign.

You don't want to artificially make your work sound harder than it was.

Instead, focus on:

What was the problem?

Why did it matter?

What did you decide to do?

What was the result?

I also think your university comparison is hurting you

You said:

"It has been relatively easy compared to my school work at university."

I actually think that's very common for technically capable people early in their careers.

University often gives you problems where the explicit objective is:

"Solve this difficult problem."

Professional software development is often:

"Make this annoying thing stop happening."

😂

The second one can feel intellectually insignificant.

But professional engineering isn't primarily about solving difficult puzzles.

It's about consistently making systems more reliable, maintainable, understandable, efficient, and useful.

You shouldn't undervalue that.

There's also something interesting about your personality here

From our technical conversations, you tend to be very analytical.

You often want to understand the underlying mechanism:

"Why does SQL Server do this?"

"Why is this a Remote Scan?"

"What exactly does merge-base do?"

"What branch is actually checked out?"

That's good.

But it may mean that when an interviewer asks:

"Tell me about something you're proud of."

your brain immediately starts searching for:

"What is the most technically impressive thing I have accomplished?"

And because nothing immediately qualifies, your brain goes blank.

Instead, your interview brain needs a different search algorithm:

"What problem did I encounter → what did I do → what changed?"

I'd actually prepare 5 stories

You don't need to memorize answers to 50 questions.

I'd prepare five work stories that you know extremely well.

For you, I'd probably want stories covering:

1. Technical problem

Something where you investigated a difficult technical issue.

For example, SQL performance/debugging.

2. Improvement

Something you automated or made more efficient.

Azure Pipelines / PowerShell / migration processes could work here.

3. Ownership

Something where you noticed a problem and took responsibility for fixing it rather than simply doing what you were told.

4. Failure / mistake

Something that didn't go as expected and what you learned.

This is extremely useful for behavioral interviews.

5. People / leadership

Something involving helping another developer, training someone, coordinating work, or your Tech Lead responsibilities.

You can then reuse these five stories for a huge number of questions.

For example

Imagine the interviewer asks:

"What is something you are proud of?"

You don't need some dramatic story.

You could structure it:

"One thing I'm particularly proud of was improving X."

Then:

"Before the change, we had Y problem."

Then:

"I looked into it and found Z."

Then:

"I decided to..."

Then:

"As a result..."

And finally:

"What I liked about this was that it wasn't just solving the immediate problem—it made the process easier for the rest of the team."

That's a very solid engineering answer.

You may be judging your answer while you're giving it.

The interviewer doesn't know that your internal standard is "this must be harder than my university projects."

They just see someone who can't identify an accomplishment.

That's unfortunate because the underlying reality may be completely different.
